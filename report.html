Executive summary

What Syracuse shows

Syracuse's college-town core — Marshall Street, University Hill, and the University Neighborhood — is a mature version of the conditions Collegetown faces: a large, tax-exempt university that owns much of its surroundings; a rental market split between local landlords and national student-housing capital; a recent form-based zoning rewrite; and a regional economy now reshaped by an external demand shock. This study answers the assignment's guiding questions for that core and draws evidence-linked implications for Collegetown.

Five findings

1
Institutional ownership is decisive. Syracuse University owns about 69% of the tight campus core's assessed value and roughly 42% across the four study tracts, and is largely property-tax-exempt; the city receives a negotiated SIDA PILOT (~$4.26M, FY2024) rather than tax. About 53% of the citywide property base is exempt.
2
The zoning was rewritten to a form-based code. ReZone (adopted June 2023) replaced Euclidean zoning with form/character-based mixed-use districts, reduced or removed parking minimums in several cases, and set the first mandatory affordability requirement for a large Upstate city (10% for 20–75-unit projects).
3
The housing market runs on three ownership tiers. Subdivided 1–3-family houses (~47% of core parcels, low owner-occupancy) held by local landlords; a small number of high-value purpose-built student-housing towers held by out-of-state capital (Landmark, American Campus, BVK/Peak); and the university itself.
4
An external shock is driving the market. Amid Micron's ~$100B fab planned ~20 miles away, county for-sale inventory fell roughly 75–83%, and University Hill is now the most expensive neighborhood in the city (~$2,503/mo).
5
Governance is fragmented. There is no single district manager. A thin Crouse-Marshall BID, the University Hill Corporation, the University Neighborhood Preservation Association, and SU's community-relations office share the field, split physically by the I-81 town-gown line.

Every claim in this report carries an evidence tag — V verified against a primary source, R reported by a credible secondary source, E estimated — and a source. Implications for Collegetown (final section) are inferences, marked as such.

2a5Affordable housing complete

2a7Other controls complete

The legacy 5-unrelated-person 'Family' cap, the Certificate of Suitability and the University Neighborhood Special District were all repealed and are absent from the current ordinance. The replacement control is the Commercial Conversion (Section 3.3A(5)): converting a 1-/2-unit dwelling or adding bedrooms triggers Major Site Plan Review, per-bedroom parking and escalating common-area minimums (100/250/300 sf), with no variance permitted. A rental registry (GO 16-2007) also applies.

2bHave regs changed? complete

2cConnection to Urban Morphology (§1) complete

The code maps onto physical form: PID's no-height-cap lets the Hill tower (SU's 13-story hotel and 4-6-story dorms sit one lot from 40-ft R1 blocks) while MX-4 ratifies Marshall St's continuous street wall and permits 8-9 stories. Intensity is set by form plus set-aside rather than FAR, pushing product toward taller, lot-line-to-lot-line, near-parkingless MX-4/MX-5 buildings and throttled bedroom-conversions in R1/R2, with the 40-ft/step-down-within-100-ft-of-R1 rule as the morphological seam. Parking-minimum elimination and required underground utilities in MX-4/MX-5 favor continuous street walls over surface lots, and historic geography is largely honorific with weak local design review.

Synthesis · Precedent

Implications for Collegetown

Each item is an inference drawn from the Syracuse evidence above — not a verified fact about Collegetown. Each states the pattern, cites the Syracuse evidence, and notes where the analogy holds or breaks.

Inference  A form-based code rewrite is the main lever on density and affordability
The zoning code sets the district's built form. Syracuse shows a city can replace Euclidean zoning with a form-based mixed-use system that permits mid-rise by-right and carries a mandatory inclusionary requirement. A code rewrite, rather than project-by-project variances, is therefore the main tool available for changing both density and affordability.
What Syracuse shows: ReZone replaced the old ordinance with a form-based Mixed-Use (MX) framework; the commercial core sits in MX-3/MX-4 with by-right height. The rewrite bundled parking-minimum reductions and exemptions with a 10% affordability set-aside on 20–75-unit projects — the first mandatory affordability rule for a large Upstate city.

Where it holds / breaks: Collegetown is governed by the City of Ithaca's code, not Syracuse's. The transferable lesson is the mechanism (form-based + inclusionary), not the specific district parameters, which reflect Syracuse's market and politics.
Inference  A dominant tax-exempt university reshapes the municipal tax base
Where one university owns most of the core's assessed value, the municipal tax base is correspondingly narrow, the city relies on a negotiated PILOT or voluntary payment rather than property tax, and the exemption becomes a standing fiscal grievance. Where it holds / breaks: The 69% figure is boundary-dependent (42%–70.9% depending on how the core is drawn). Cornell/Ithaca's exemption share and any PILOT arrangements differ and must be checked directly — this identifies the pattern to test, not Collegetown's numbers.
Inference  National capital enters a tight market and concentrates value in a few towers
Once a college-town rental market tightens, out-of-state purpose-built student housing (PBSH) capital moves in. A handful of towers end up holding a disproportionate share of value and reset the rent structure onto a per-bed basis.
What Syracuse shows: Purpose-built apartments are only ~11% of core parcels but hold most of the value. The recent towers are national/institutional LLCs — Theory (BVK / Bayerische parent), Park Point (SU-owned land, American Campus operator) — a small tier of out-of-state owners with few parcels but large value.
  • V Purpose-built apartments are ~11% of parcels but hold most of the value, because each purpose-built student-housing (PBSH) tower is one large parcel. ~11 % of parcels — City of Syracuse assessment roll (data.syr.gov, 2025)
  • V PBSH building - Theory Syracuse, 919 E Genesee; owner LLC BVK SYRACUSE PROP OWNER LLC; parent Bayerische Versorgungskammer (German pension) via Blue Vista, PeakMade/Scion management; HQ Atlanta/Chicago; 245 units / 601 beds; assessed $49.6M. — City of Syracuse assessment roll (data.syr.gov, 2025); 07_housing_ownership_VERIFIED.md
  • V Ownership tier 3 - regional/national institutional capital (out-of-state LLCs): few parcels but the highest-value private assets (the PBSH towers). — City of Syracuse assessment roll (data.syr.gov, 2025); 07_housing_ownership_VERIFIED.md
Where it holds / breaks: This is a market-stage observation. Whether Collegetown is at the same point depends on its current development pipeline and remaining land — a tightening market is the precondition, not a guarantee.
Inference  The house stock consolidates into a low-owner-occupancy, code-violation-heavy submarket
The 1–3-family houses around a campus tend to consolidate under a few landlords, fall to low owner-occupancy, and carry high code-violation counts. This is a common condition of student-neighborhood housing, not specific to Syracuse.
What Syracuse shows: 1–3-family houses are about 47% of core parcels; at most ~25% of core residential parcels are plausibly owner-occupied. Single landlords hold dozens of parcels apiece — one operator with 60+ properties, another group with ~53 parcels — and 15 college-town streets carry 2,568 recorded code violations.
  • V 1-3-family houses (the subdivided per-bed student stock) make up ~46.6% of the 755 core parcels. 46.6 % of parcels — City of Syracuse assessment roll (data.syr.gov, 2025)
  • V Ownership-based tenure proxy: at most ~25% of the 559 core residential parcels are plausibly owner-occupied. <=25 % of 559 core residential parcels — City of Syracuse assessment roll (data.syr.gov, 2025)
  • V Ben Tupper (Tupper Property Management / 'Rent From Ben') owns 'over 60 properties' in the university neighborhood. 60+ properties properties — dailyorange.com/2026/03/rent-from-ben-hated-landlords-one-of-syracuses-biggest/ (Daily Orange, Mar 2026)
Where it holds / breaks: Owner-occupancy here is a mailing-address proxy, and violation counts reflect enforcement activity as well as conditions. Collegetown's exact figures need its own parcel roll and code data, but the direction is well-established.
Inference  A regional demand shock can overwhelm the college-town market
A college town's housing market is not insulated from its region. A nearby megaproject can create demand that exceeds student demand and pushes up rents across the area.
What Syracuse shows: Micron's ~$100B fab about 20 miles away coincides with county for-sale inventory falling ~75–83% and University Hill becoming the most expensive neighborhood in the city (~$2,503/mo). Regional homelessness rose about 150% since 2019 over the same period.
  • V Investment driver: Micron (~$100B fab in Clay). ~100 $B — 00_FACT_AUDIT; finance.syr.edu; governor.ny.gov (drivers synthesis)
  • V Onondaga County for-sale inventory fell ~75% on an annual-average basis (Micron demand shock). ~75% for-sale listings — SECTION_10 forces file B5
  • V University Hill average rent ~$2,503/mo — the most expensive neighborhood in the city (Aug 2026). $2,503/mo $/mo — https://www.rentcafe.com/average-rent-market-trends/us/ny/syracuse/
Where it holds / breaks: Co-movement is not proof of causation, and Ithaca has no equivalent megaproject on the horizon. The transferable point is a college town's exposure to regional demand, not Micron specifically.
Inference  The university may vertically integrate its commercial Main Street
A university with the balance sheet to do so may buy up its commercial Main Street, shifting control of college-town retail from independent merchants to the institution.
What Syracuse shows: SU is buying the Marshall Street retail spine — March 2025 purchases of Lucy Blu, Manny's, and Mediterranean Combo — while the older Crouse-Marshall BID that manages the strip is real but thin. SU's purchases have run about 6× the City's assessed values, so the roll understates what it is paying.
  • V SU is buying the Marshall St retail spine — March 2025 purchases of Lucy Blu, Manny's, and Mediterranean Combo (~$14M+, 7-8 properties across two parcels), with 'no immediate plans to change' the spaces; SU becomes emerging landlord of the commercial core in addition to CMBID board member. ~$14M+ USD — https://dailyorange.com/2025/03/syracuse-university-expands-properties-on-marshall-street/

  • V SU's own recent purchases ran ~6x the City's assessed valuations. ~6x assessed ratio (SU purchase price to assessed value) — spectrumlocalnews.com 2025/03/25 (Syracuse poised for first property reevaluation in decades); syracuse.com/news/2025/03
Where it holds / breaks: This is an active, incomplete process. How much SU ends up owning, and to what use, is not yet known — watch it as an unfolding pattern rather than a settled outcome.
Inference  University housing policy sets the off-campus market's timing and volume
A residency requirement directly controls off-campus demand. Requiring underclassmen to live on campus concentrates that demand into the upper years, which sets how much and when the private market absorbs.
What Syracuse shows: SU requires first- and second-year students to live in campus housing, so the off-campus market effectively opens in junior year. The resulting off-campus stock splits between cheap house-conversions (~$700–900/bed) and premium purpose-built product.
  • V SU requires first- and second-year students to live in campus housing; the off-campus market opens junior year and concentrates in this district plus Westcott. — findmyplace.co/blog/syracuse-off-campus-housing/
  • V SU requires undergrads to live on campus two years; the off-campus market opens junior year, concentrating demand in University Hill / University Neighborhood / Westcott. — SECTION_10 10a-2 (SU housing policy)
  • V Older house-conversions rent ~$700-$900/bed. ~$700-$900/bed $/bed/mo — SECTION_7 7e-2 (rentcafe.com; nccnewsonline.com)
Where it holds / breaks: Cornell's own residency rules (e.g., the North Campus requirement) differ. The mechanism transfers — policy sets the demand curve — but the specific timing and split do not.
Inference  Governance is layered, and a hard town-gown edge can be physical
College-town governance is rarely one body. Expect a small BID, a residents' preservation association, the university's community-relations office, and city planning to divide responsibility — and a physical barrier can turn the town-gown boundary into a literal one.
What Syracuse shows: I-81 forms a near-literal town-gown line in Syracuse — institutions and the hospital hill to the east, a high-poverty city to the west. A residents' owner-occupancy organization (UNPA, est. 1990) sits east of campus, and day-to-day district management runs through a thin Crouse-Marshall BID.
  • V I-81 forms a near-literal town-gown line (university/hospitals east; high-poverty city west), rooted in mid-century redlining and destruction of the 15th Ward. — SECTION_10 10a-8 (structural divide)
  • V UNPA (University Neighborhood Preservation Association, est. 1990) is a residents' owner-occupancy org east of campus; no findable IRS 990 (volunteer/sub-$50k). — ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer search (zero matches)
  • V Crouse-Marshall BID District Management Association: 501(c)(4), EIN 05-0533961, FYE Jun 30; ~34-40 properties; tax-exempt since Dec 2004; digitized 990s 2011-2025. — https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/050533961; MASTER/FACTBASE §4
Where it holds / breaks: Collegetown's specific governance actors and any physical edges are its own. This names the categories to look for — district manager, residents' org, university liaison, hard boundary — not the specific bodies.
Method & sources

Evidence, method & caveats

Method

Findings are compiled from primary sources — City of Syracuse ordinance PDFs and Planning Commission staff reports, the City's 2025 Q3 open-data parcel roll, U.S. Census / ACS, IRS Form 990 filings, NYSDOT, and the City Auditor — supplemented by credible secondary reporting. Parcel ownership, valuation, zoning, vacancy, and land use are read directly from the 8,431-parcel roll for the four core tracts and their surroundings. 44 claims found false, stale, or mis-framed during the audit were corrected and are excluded here.

Two caveats that shape every number

Assessed value ≠ market value. Syracuse's last citywide revaluation before this cycle was in the mid-1990s; its first reval in roughly 30 years was underway in 2024–25. Roll values understate the market — SU's own purchases have run about 6× assessed — so ownership shares are more reliable than dollar levels.

The "SU owns most of it" figure is boundary-dependent. Syracuse University's share of assessed value is about 70.9% in the campus-heart tract (43.02), ~46% across the campus core excluding residential, and ~42% across all four study tracts. This report states the boundary with the figure.
